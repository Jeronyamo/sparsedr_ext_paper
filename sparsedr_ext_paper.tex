\begin{abstract}
3D reconstruction is one of the fundamental problems in computer vision and graphics. One effective approach to this task is differentiable rendering, which aims to make the stages of the rendering pipeline differentiable, enabling reconstruction via gradient-based optimization. A separate line of work focuses on differentiable physically based rendering, allowing the simultaneous recovery of both geometry and material properties of scene objects. The latest methods of this category rely on signed distance functions (SDFs), a flexible representation suited for geometry reconstruction. However, existing SDF-based methods typically employ dense SDF grids, resulting in high memory consumption, slow sphere-tracing-based ray traversal, and a computationally expensive redistancing step that propagates updated SDF values across the entire grid. The proposed method addresses these challenges by leveraging a sparse brick set (SBS) representation, a Newton-based method for ray-surface intersection, and two novel variants of the redistancing algorithm adapted to sparse SDF representations and the specific requirements of differentiable rendering. This approach overcomes the aforementioned limitations, improves both reconstruction speed and quality, and reduces model size growth during optimization by an order of magnitude.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

3D reconstruction is a core challenge in computer vision, aiming to infer the parameters of real-world objects or scenes from images, video, or sensor measurements. It is closely related to inverse rendering. While forward rendering involves generating 2D images from a 3D scene, the goal of inverse rendering is to reconstruct scene objects from a set of images assumed to be produced under a specific rendering process. Significant progress has been made in this area over the past decade, driven by the rapid development of differentiable rendering. Differentiable rendering assumes that all stages of the rendering pipeline are differentiable, thereby enabling the use of gradient-based optimization for scene reconstruction. Consequently, gradients of the loss function -- calculated based on the difference between the reconstructed and reference images -- can be propagated to the parameters being optimized (such as material properties and geometry), allowing them to converge toward the parameters of the reference scene during optimization process.

In recent years, Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) become widely used in 3D reconstruction \cite{ref-lin-2020,ref-sommer-2022,ref-slavcheva-2016} and differentiable rendering \cite{DRSDF2022,RelaxBoundary}. The smooth and continuous representation of surfaces provided by the SDFs is the main reasons behind this popularity. This property is also useful for the inverse CAD design problem \cite{invCAD1,invCAD2}, where the representation must be differentiable with respect to the model parameters. When modeling real-world objects, particularly in CAD systems, it is often unnecessary to compute SDF values throughout the entire 3D space. The primary interest lies in the thin region surrounding the surface, specifically, in the area where the absolute value of the SDF does not exceed a certain threshold distance. In \cite{RelaxBoundary}, this region is termed the "relaxed boundary." Sparse SDFs store and process data only within such regions.

In this paper, we proposes a differentiable rendering method for sparse SDF representations. Our work builds upon the relaxed boundary approach~\cite{RelaxBoundary}, yet we have redesigned every stage of the optimization algorithm. To address the high computational cost of the redistancing procedure, we propose an approach that outperforms the existing algorithm in terms of efficiency. This procedure significantly affects optimization time, as it is executed at each optimization step to update values following distance changes; since the grid ceases to represent a valid distance function, all distance values at the grid nodes must be recalculated. Although the core ideas used in this work were presented at the IJCAI’2026 conference \cite{ijcai2026}, the results reported in this paper are significantly expanded and advanced. In particular, the main algorithms were optimized, increasing the speedup of the reconstruction time from a factor of 2-3.5 to an order of magnitude. The size of the test dataset was doubled. Additional experiments and comparative studies were conducted, including an ablation study that was not presented at the conference.  

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A differentiable rendering algorithm for sparse SDF representations that outperforms the baseline method in speed and reduces the growth rate of the optimized model's size by an order of magnitude.
    \item An algorithm for distance recalculation in sparse SDF representations (redistancing algorithm); its local version achieves an order-of-magnitude speedup while maintaining reconstruction quality. The local version of this algorithm is best suited for the differentiable rendering of sparse SDFs. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Differentiable rendering}

Numerous methods have been proposed to provide differentiable rendering algorithms for various types of scene representations. These methods can be broadly divided into two categories: neural and non-neural approaches. The former includes NeRF \cite{NeRF} and other neural rendering techniques that reconstruct representations such as point clouds or signed distance functions (SDFs) \cite{NeUS2, NeuAngelo}. The latter category includes methods such as Gaussian splatting \cite{3DGS, 2DGS} and mesh splatting \cite{MeshS}.

However, when both geometry and material properties must be considered (for instance, in physically based rendering) most of these methods require additional post-processing. First, their outputs must be converted into representations compatible with path tracing pipelines, such as SDFs or meshes. Although approaches for such conversion exist (for example, \cite{BakedSDF, NeRFMeshing} for NeRF and \cite{SuGaR} for Gaussian splatting), quality degradation during conversion is unavoidable. Mesh splatting methods address this issue by directly reconstructing meshes; however, they introduce another limitation: while they allow for texture recovery, they do not support the recovery of material properties. At the same time, there exists a class of methods specifically designed to obtain accurate gradients for ray tracing in physically based rendering.

\subsection{Differentiable physically-based rendering}

The edge sampling method \cite{EdgeSampling} was introduced in 2018 as a differentiable rendering method applicable to path tracing. The proposed approach accounts for both primary and secondary visibility and, for the first time, enables correct differentiation with respect to arbitrary scene parameters. The study demonstrated that when differentiating the rendering integral with respect to parameters defining an object's shape, the integrand becomes discontinuous, and the locations of these discontinuities depend on the aforementioned parameters. In such cases, the derivative of the integral is obtained using the Reynolds transport theorem:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \frac{d}{d \theta} \int_{\Omega_\theta}{f(\omega;\theta) d\omega} =
        \\
        \int_{\Omega_{\theta}}{\frac{d}{d \theta} f(\omega;\theta) d\omega} + \int_{\partial \Omega_{\theta}}{(\partial_\theta \omega \cdot \textbf{n}) f(\omega;\theta) d\omega}
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}

The first term is referred to as the interior integral, while the second is the boundary integral. The difficulty in evaluating the boundary integral lies in the fact that, for any viewpoint, the measure of its domain is zero, making it impossible to sample using Monte Carlo methods. The work \cite{EdgeSampling} uses explicit sampling: silhouette edges of the mesh are identified first, followed by uniform sampling along them. For polygonal meshes, this operation is computationally expensive. Therefore, subsequent studies have proposed methods for evaluating the boundary integral without explicit boundary sampling. The reparameterization method \cite{Reparam} handles discontinuities locally, whereas the warped-area sampling method \cite{WAS} transforms the boundary integral into a volumetric one, enabling the reuse of samples obtained for the interior integral.

The methods discussed above are theoretically independent of the chosen object representation. In practice, however, they are applied to the reconstruction of polygonal meshes. Under these conditions, optimization based on this representation faces several fundamental limitations that significantly complicate the reconstruction task. These limitations are analyzed in the work \cite{Nicolet}. The primary issue with mesh-based representations is the inability to modify the topology (for example, the surface genus) of the initial approximation. Furthermore, the optimization process can lead to irreversible self-intersections and a non-uniform distribution of primitives: regions with high geometric detail may lack sufficient elements to accurately capture the shape. The modifications proposed in \cite{Nicolet} partially mitigate the latter two issues; however, self-intersections persist, and the topological constraint remains a significant obstacle to effective optimization.

\subsection{Differentiable rendering of SDFs}

Since 2021, a number of studies have been devoted to differentiable rendering using SDFs defineed on a regular grid, where signed distance values are stored at the grid nodes. For any point in space, the function returns the distance to the surface by interpolating the grid values; a negative value indicates that the point lies inside the represented object.

The work \cite{HanssonSoderlund} analyzes existing SDF representations and proposed new variants, along with techniques for finding intersection. In addition to the standard sphere tracing algorithm, two new methods for ray-voxel intersection are presented. To the best of our knowledge, these representations have not previously been used in differentiable rendering.

}

In \cite{DRSDF}, the reparameterization method was applied to signed distance functions for the first time. The study compared two approaches: the direct application of the method \cite{Reparam} and a modified variant adapted specifically for SDFs. In parallel, \cite{NeuDRSDF} proposed a reparameterization technique for neural SDFs that eliminates the need for masking during optimization.

Finally, within the relaxed boundary method \cite{RelaxBoundary}, the authors further develop the concept of reparameterization as a transformation of a surface integral to a volume integral. The proposed approach to differentiable rendering leverages the properties of distance functions, replacing the boundary integral with an integral over a “relaxed boundary,” defined as a thin region surrounding the object's silhouette. The use of SDFs enables the identification of points within this region during ray tracing, as the distance function value at these points falls below a predefined threshold determined by a hyperparameter.

The described methods advance the differentiable, physics-based rendering by introducing a simple approach for evaluating boundary integrals for signed distance functions -- a representation that is more flexible for surface reconstruction than meshes. However, this approach has several drawbacks. First, the model size grows rapidly: during optimization, the grid resolution increases progressively, and at each upsampling step its size expands eightfold, significantly limiting the method's scalability. Second, rendering remains slow due to the use of sphere tracing. Third, even with local changes in distances near the surface, existing methods employ a computationally expensive redistancing algorithm. This procedure is performed at every optimization step to update values following distance changes, as the grid ceases to represent a valid distance function; consequently, all distance values in the grid require recalculation.

\section{Method}

Our method is named SparseDR (Differentiable Rendering for Sparse SDF). Its main idea is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{figSparseDR}. The reconstruction process proceeds over several epochs. At a fixed SDF resolution, the optimization process converges within a few hundred iterations. To further improve reconstruction quality, the grid resolution must be increased. We treat this as a division into optimization epochs. Each epoch, described in Algorithm \ref{alg:OptimEpoch}, consists of several hundred iterations, comprising the following steps. First, a batch of images is rendered using reference viewpoints with gradient accumulation. Next, regularization is applied to the distance values. A loss function representing the discrepancy between the synthesized and reference images is then computed, and backpropagation is performed. At the end of each iteration, a local redistancing procedure is carried out. Between epochs, as shown in the Algorithm \ref{alg:OptimFull}, sparsification and upsampling of the signed distance function (SDF) are performed, followed by a global redistancing calculation.

\subsection{Rendering and Gradient Evaluation}

Our reconstruction method employs the Sparse Brick Set (SBS) representation \cite{HanssonSoderlund}. This is a hybrid of hierarchical and regular structures: a BVH tree with leaf nodes storing small, regular SDF grids, which enables efficient culling of nodes that do not contain the object's surface. A ray first traverses the BVH tree; upon intersecting a leaf node, it proceeds to intersect the voxels of the corresponding brick. The ray continues moving through bricks along its trajectory until it either reaches the surface or exhausts the bricks in its path.

In addition to the representation itself, our method utilizes two ray-surface intersection techniques proposed for SBS -- specifically, the Newton's method and an analytical approach. To correctly compute the gradient, we apply the relaxed boundary method \cite{RelaxBoundary}, adapted to these intersection techniques. Both, Newton's method and analytic approach compute ray-voxel intersections under the assumption that the SDF along the ray within the voxel can be represented as a cubic polynomial; the only difference between them lies in the root-finding procedure.
We look for a point on the ray that is as close as possible to the surface. On the other hand, the key property of a relaxed boundary point is that it corresponds to a local minimum of the SDF along the ray. Therefore, the task of detecting such points within a voxel reduces to finding the corresponding root of a quadratic equation. However, when trilinear interpolation is used, the local minimum is often reached at the voxel boundaries, and these boundary cases are also handled separately.

The algorithm is implemented in C++ using Vulkan API without automatic differentiation; all gradients are computed analytically. To support an arbitrary number of samples per pixel, the shader responsible for rendering the optimized model and computing the boundary integral accumulates gradients every 16 samples, using an intermediate color estimate rather than waiting for the final pixel color to be calculated. This approach is necessitated by the fact that information about the intersected voxel must be stored for each sample, while the accuracy of the gradient estimate depends on the pixel color obtained after sampling. At the same time, the number of samples is user-defined and not known in advance. We assume that 16 samples are sufficient to produce a color close to the final value, and therefore store voxel information in a fixed-size stack.

\subsection{Redistancing}

\Figure[t](topskip=0pt, botskip=0pt, midskip=0pt)[width=0.9\linewidth]{Resources/Graphic9c1.eps} {
    Extrapolation step scheme, the yellow side of the brick is extrapolated from the neighbours' sides (green). a) search for neighboring blocks; b) extrapolation of side values from each neighbor.  
    \label{figNeighborBricks}
}

After a gradient descent step, the updated distance values, strictly speaking, cease to be a signed distance function (SDF): they no longer correspond to the true distances to the surface, and the grid no longer satisfies the Eikonal equation. To restore this property, the baseline method recompute distances at grid nodes (redistancing) using the fast sweeping method \cite{RedistFSM}. Redistansing for sparse representations is more complex: when recomputing distances within a specific model brick, it is additionally necessary to identify and account for neighboring bricks that are not directly connected via stored distances (i.e., intermediate bricks may be absent, yet such neighbors still influence the correct distance values).

Our global redistancing is the redistancing procedure for SBS structure. To ensure correct distance computation, an extrapolation step is introduced: for each brick face that does not adjoin another brick (i.e., lacks a direct neighbor), non-direct neighboring bricks are identified, and the distances for that face are extrapolated from the nearest face of those neighbors (see Fig. \ref{figNeighborBricks}). The minimum absolute values are then selected among the current and all computed distances. The redistancing procedure is parallelized across bricks.

One of the advantages of voxel-based intersection search methods is that they are less sensitive to the accuracy of distance values than sphere tracing. As a result, a voxel can be safely ignored if all its distance values exceed a relaxed epsilon; in this case, neither an intersection point nor a relaxed boundary point can be found within it. Consequently, precise distance values within bricks that do not contain the surface are of limited importance; in particular, there is no need to recompute distances for such bricks.

Local redistancing is performed frequently, after each iteration of the method. So we have further optimized the algorithm. The first optimization restricts distance recomputation to only bricks that contain the surface. The second optimization assumes that distances within surface-containing bricks depend only on the surface within that specific brick, disregarding distances in neighboring bricks. This assumption simplifies implementation and improves data locality without significantly compromising reconstruction quality.  Fig. \ref{figRedistTypes} illustrates the concept behind this local redistancing optimization. Optimization of local algorithm significantly accelerates the redistancing (Table \ref{tabGlobLoc}) for an iteration performed several hundred times per epoch.

\subsection{Upsampling and Sparsification}

When the optimization process converges within one epoch , grid upsampling (increasing of SDF resolution) is required to further improve the reconstruction quality. The final model quality depends directly on the number of epochs can be performed while maintaining a sufficiently high iteration speed and an acceptable data representation size. In the SBS approach, resolution is increased by introducing new distance values and subdividing each existing brick into eight smaller ones; each new brick contains the same number of distance samples as the original but represents a spatial region eight times smaller. 

A sparsification step is also introduced: bricks are added and removed so that the final representation consists only of bricks containing surface segments, along with a one-brick padding around them. Sparsification is always performed between epochs, but it can also be applied during an epoch. This is necessary when reconstructing models with fine details, as such details cannot be reconstructed in early epochs due to the coarse grid, and in later epochs the bricks corresponding to these details can be removed.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{The optimization epoch algorithm.}
    \label{alg:OptimEpoch}

    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
\item[] \textbf{function} OptimEpoch($SDF_{init}, I_{ref}, M_{ref}, n_{ref}, n_{iter}$)
    \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow SDF_{init}$
    \STATE $S_{mark} \leftarrow $ MarkSurfaceBricks($SDF_{res}$)
    \FOR{$i = 1$ \TO $n_{iter}$}
        \STATE $k \leftarrow $ SelectView($n_{ref}$)
        \STATE $I_{res}, \partial SDF \leftarrow $ Render($SDF_{res}, M_{ref}[k]$)
        \STATE $L_{MSE} \leftarrow $ MSE($I_{res}, I_{ref}[k]$)
        \STATE $\partial SDF \leftarrow $ dMSE($I_{res}, I_{ref}[k], L_{MSE}, \partial SDF$)
        \STATE $L_{REG}, \partial SDF_{reg} \leftarrow $ RegularizeLocal($SDF_{res}, S_{mark}$)
        \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow$ Backpropagate($SDF_{res}, \partial SDF, \partial SDF_{reg}$)
        \STATE $S_{mark} \leftarrow $ MarkSurfaceBricks($SDF_{res}$)
        \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow $ RedistanceLocal($SDF_{res}, S_{mark}$)
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $SDF_{res}$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{The outer optimization loop.}
    \label{alg:OptimFull}

    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
\item[] \textbf{function} Optimize($I_{ref}, M_{ref}, n_{ref}$)
    \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow SDF_{sphere}$
    \FOR{$i = 1$ \TO $n_{epoch}$}
        \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow $ OptimEpoch($SDF_{res}, I_{ref}, M_{ref}, n_{ref}, N_{iter}[i]$)
        \STATE $S_{mark} \leftarrow $ MarkSurfaceBricks($SDF_{res}$)
        \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow $ Sparsify($SDF_{res}, S_{mark}$)
        \IF{$i < n_{epoch}$}
            \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow $ Upsample($SDF_{res}$)
        \ENDIF
        \STATE $SDF_{res} \leftarrow $ RedistanceGlobal($SDF_{res}$)
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $SDF_{res}$
    \end{algorithmic}

\end{algorithm}

% На рис. \ref{figSTNt} приведено сравнение времени выполнения семи эпох при использовании двух методов поиска пересечений луча с SBS: трассировки сферами и метода Ньютона. При дальнейшем увеличении числа вокселей возрастает количество участков SBS, с которыми требуется вычислить пересечение луча, что увеличивает суммарные затраты на выполнение итераций; из-за этого на дальнейших эпохах преимущество метода Ньютона может сократиться.
Figure \ref{figSTNt} compares the runtime over seven epochs for two ray-SBS intersection methods: sphere tracing and the Newton method. The runtime of sphere tracing depends not only on the number of iterations but also on the geometric complexity of the model, since finding an intersection also requires traversing the BVH. As the model size increases, the number of nodes that must be traversed grows, leading to higher computational cost.

Newton's method exhibits shorter runtime than sphere tracing over the first seven epochs. This is because, at these SBS sizes, the number of voxels intersected by rays is still relatively small, so finding an intersection with a small number of iterations is faster than sphere tracing. As the number of voxels further increases, the number of SBS regions for which ray intersections must be computed also grows, raising the total cost per iteration; as a result, the advantage of the Newton method may diminish in later epochs.

During reconstruction, additional tools can be employed to improve the accuracy of model reconstruction. First, object masks can be used for the reference images. Mask sensitivity is specified for each epoch and is set to 0 by default. During this step, bricks are removed and added depending on their proximity to the surface, allowing the represented region to grow where significant surface is added. Figure \ref{figAblSparsify} shows examples of reconstruction without and with additional sparsification, along with a reference image for clarity. In the first case, the helicopter's propeller failed to reconstruct because it is thin; as a result, its coarse version did not recover in the early epochs, and the bricks that should represent its region of space were removed. With additional sparsification, these bricks, and subsequently the propeller itself, were recovered at a later epoch.

\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we propose SparseDR, an efficient reconstruction method based on differentiable rendering of sparse signed distance fields (SDFs). Our method achieves high accuracy through the high effective resolution of the representation while maintaining a controlled memory footprint by storing data sparsely. We demonstrate that sparse SDF representations can serve as first-class geometric representations for reconstruction applications with direct ray-traced rendering, enabling fast ray-traced visualization of such representations in interactive 3D applications.

The current implementation focuses on geometry reconstruction and direct illumination. It does not yet model secondary rays or estimate material properties. However, the underlying architecture can be extended toward a fully differentiable path tracer. Future work will incorporate secondary rays and more complete light transport simulation to improve reconstruction in scenes where indirect illumination affects the observed images. We also plan to jointly reconstruct geometry, light sources, and material properties. Although these extensions require further development, the present results show that sparse SDF-based differentiable rendering is a suitable foundation for inverse rendering systems that recover geometry, materials, and illumination.
